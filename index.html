Step 1 of 6

A too-good offer

It starts with a message. An agent promises a place at a famous university and a full scholarship. It sounds like a dream.

The warning sign

No one can promise admission or a visa. Only the university decides who gets in, and only the embassy decides the visa.

Do this now Do not reply or pay yet. Paste the message into the free check first.

The free check

Agent message check: paste a WhatsApp, SMS or email message. It looks for common scam patterns, like big promises and secrecy. The text never leaves your phone.

Step 2 of 6

A fake admission or scholarship letter

Next comes a letter. It has a logo, a stamp and a signature, so it looks real. But anyone can make a letter look real.

The warning sign

Watch for a free email address like Gmail, spelling mistakes, a fee to pay within hours, or a request to keep it secret.

Do this now Log in to the university's own applicant portal yourself. Check that the same offer is shown there.

The free check

University check: see if the university is on an official register, like the UK student sponsor register or the Australian register for overseas students (CRICOS).

Step 3 of 6

People pretending to be officials

Then someone calls. They say they are from the embassy, the university or the police. They know your name, and they sound official.

The warning sign

Government offices do not phone you to demand money, and they do not threaten arrest to make you pay.

Do this now Hang up. Find the office's number on its own official website, and call back yourself.

The free check

Email or link check: paste the email address or link. It compares it with the university's real website and looks for lookalike tricks.

Step 4 of 6

Pressure to pay fast

Now the clock starts. "Pay today or you lose your seat." "The scholarship closes tonight." They want you to rush.

The warning sign

Rushing is the scammer's best tool. Real deadlines are written on the university's own website.

Do this now Stop for one day. Show everything to a parent, a teacher or someone you trust.

Step 5 of 6

Payment to a personal account

They send bank details. The account is in a person's name, or it is a mobile wallet. They say it is faster this way.

The warning sign

Universities take fees through their own portal, their own bank account or a payment service linked from their own website. Not through a person's account, a mobile wallet, cash or crypto.

Do this now Pay only through the official portal, or to the bank details on the university's own website.

    Know the common scams

    Read the 8 most common tricks

    1. "Guaranteed admission" or "guaranteed visa"

    Only the university decides admission and only the embassy or immigration authority decides a visa. Anyone who guarantees either is lying or planning to use false documents, which can lead to a visa ban.

    2. Fake offer letters, CAS (Confirmation of Acceptance for Studies), CoE (Confirmation of Enrolment) or I-20 (US Certificate of Eligibility)

    A document can look perfect and still be forged. Software cannot make a document genuine. Log in to the official applicant portal yourself, or contact the admissions office using details from the university's own website.

    3. Paying into the wrong account

    Scammers send "updated bank details" or ask for payment to a personal account, JazzCash/Easypaisa, hawala, crypto or gift cards. Universities take fees through their own portal, their own bank account, or an official payment service linked from their own website (for example Flywire or Convera). Check the beneficiary name and country every time.

    4. Lookalike emails and websites

    oxford-admissions.com, rnonash.edu and admissions.university@gmail.com are not university addresses. Type the website address yourself. Use the "Email / link" check.

    5. "Don't tell anyone" and "decide today"

    Secrecy and pressure are the two strongest warning signs. Real deadlines are published on the university website. A genuine adviser is happy for your family or teacher to check.

    6. Agent "authorisation"

    A British Council training certificate means a counsellor completed a course; it does not mean a university authorised them. Ask the university directly whether the agent is its representative.

    7. Your passwords and one-time passwords (OTPs)

    Never give your portal password, email password or one-time codes to an agent. With them, someone can withdraw your application or accept an offer in your name.

    Before selling land, gold or a house

    Stop. Confirm admission and the exact fee amount directly with the university first. Talk to someone you trust. No legitimate deadline requires selling property within hours.

    About TaleemParakh

    TaleemParakh is a free student-protection project for Pakistani students applying abroad. No student pays for safety checks. We do not sell data, rankings, badges or agent listings, and we take no commission from anyone.

    Laiba's story

    Laiba was a bright young graduate who dreamed of studying abroad.

    According to the Federal Investigation Agency (FIA), people pretending to be officials promised her a place at a university abroad and a scholarship. The FIA says the papers were fake. It was a fake study-abroad admission and scholarship scam. It took her family's savings.

    The FIA has registered a case. The matter is now being adjudicated by the courts, so we do not comment on it.

    Laiba is no longer with us.

    What we want every student to know

    • Being scammed is never your fault. Scammers are practised at this, and they fool smart, careful people.
    • Losing money is painful, but it is not your worth. There are people who can help you take the next step.
    • You are not alone. Tell someone you trust today: a parent, an older brother or sister, a teacher or a friend.
    • Parents: if your child tells you they were scammed, thank them for telling you. Stay calm, stay close, and do not blame them.

    Privacy

    Nothing you type leaves your device. The only network requests go to this same site: the app, the guide pages (a few are saved for offline use when you first visit) and the public register file. The site is hosted on GitHub Pages, which records visitor IP addresses in its server logs, as any web host does. There are no cookies and no analytics. Your language and last tab are remembered in your browser's local storage, and the site saves a copy of itself on your device so it can work offline. Links to official websites open those sites, which have their own rules.

    Method and sources

    The checks are fixed rules, not artificial intelligence: the same input always gives the same result. Every result shows its source and date. Register files come only from the documented open-data endpoints of the UK government (gov.uk content API) and the Australian government (data.gov.au). Each edition is recorded with its address, SHA-256 fingerprint and fetch time, and a very large change stops the update from being published.
